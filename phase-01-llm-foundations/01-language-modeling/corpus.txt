A language model is a machine that assigns probabilities to text. Nothing more mystical
than that. Given some text so far, it produces a distribution over what could come next.
Every capability you associate with large models is built on top of that single trick.

The trick works because language is redundant. After the letters "probabil", almost nothing
but "i" can follow. After "the capital of France is", one answer dominates. A model that
has read enough text learns these regularities as numbers, and those numbers are the model.

Training and inference are different activities on the same object. During training, the
text is already known. The model reads a passage, predicts the next token at every position
at once, compares each prediction against the token that actually appeared, and nudges its
numbers toward the truth. Nothing is generated. The answer key is the corpus itself, which
is why this kind of learning needs no labels and can consume the open internet.

During inference the answer key is gone. The model sees a prompt, produces one distribution,
one token is chosen from it, and that token is appended to the text. Then the whole enlarged
text is fed back in and the process repeats. This loop is what autoregressive generation
means: the output of each step becomes part of the input to the next step. A model that
writes a thousand words has run a thousand times, each time predicting only one token.

Two consequences follow from the loop. First, generation is sequential and therefore slow in
a way training is not, because step ten cannot begin until step nine has chosen. Second,
mistakes compound, since a bad token stays in the context forever and every later prediction
is conditioned on it. Much of the engineering around these systems exists to manage those
two facts.

Context is whatever text the model can see when it makes a prediction. It is finite. The
model has no memory outside it, no notebook, no sense of yesterday. A conversation feels
continuous only because the entire transcript is resent on every turn, which is why long
chats cost more and why a fact that scrolls out of the window is simply gone.

The choice of what to do with a distribution is yours, not the model's. Always taking the
single most likely token is called greedy decoding, and it makes text that is correct and
lifeless and prone to loops. Sampling from the distribution restores variety at the cost of
reliability. Temperature scales the sharpness of the distribution before sampling: low
temperature concentrates probability on the leaders, high temperature flattens it and lets
unlikely tokens through. A model is a fixed function; the sampler is a dial you hold.

None of this requires a neural network. A counting model over the last few characters is
already a language model in the full sense, with a distribution, a generation loop, and a
loss you can measure. It fails not because the idea is wrong but because counting cannot
generalize: a context it has never seen tells it nothing, and the number of possible
contexts grows faster than any corpus. The transformer is an answer to that failure, not a
replacement for the framework.

Evaluation of a raw language model is refreshingly concrete. Show it text it has never seen,
ask how much probability it assigned to the tokens that actually appeared, and average the
surprise. That average is cross-entropy loss, and its exponential is perplexity, which can
be read as the effective number of choices the model felt it had at each position. A
perplexity of two means the model was about as confused as someone flipping a coin. Lower is
better, and the whole history of scaling is a history of driving this one number down on text
the model was never trained on.

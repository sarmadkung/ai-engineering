The harbour at dawn is a machine for making light complicated. Water takes the sky
apart and hands it back in pieces. The fishing boats have been out since three and the
men aboard them are not thinking about the sea, they are thinking about diesel and the
price of ice and whether the gearbox will hold another season.

My grandmother kept accounts in a ledger she never showed anyone. Columns of figures in
a hand that got smaller as the years went on, as though she were trying to fit more of
her life onto each page. When she died we found forty years of it in a drawer under the
linen, and not one entry explained.

There was a bakery on the corner then. You could tell the time by what came out of it:
rolls before six, loaves by eight, the sweet things at eleven when the women came down
from the hill. The baker had burned both forearms so often that the hair never grew back
and he wore it like a trade certificate.

Winter closed the road twice a year and nobody complained, because a closed road meant
the whole village in one room and the good brandy out. My father told the same three
stories every time and every time the room let him, which is a kind of love I did not
recognize until much later.

What I remember is not events but weather, and not weather but the way people arranged
themselves against it. Chairs pulled toward stoves. Coats worn indoors. The particular
silence of a house where someone is ill upstairs and everyone downstairs is pretending
to read.
